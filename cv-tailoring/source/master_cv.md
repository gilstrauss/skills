**The career thesis — "modern engineering underneath traditional processes."** *(Added
2026-08-30, from Gil's own stated reason for wanting the Factify CTO role.)* A one-line
framing that unifies the whole career instead of presenting it as unrelated industries:
**modern engineering underneath traditional business processes that cannot be allowed to
break.** Fourteen years of regulated real-money trading, and now legacy education systems a
million students depend on. Both are traditional processes with no tolerance for failure;
the engineering underneath them is what Gil has always supplied.

Use it for:
- **"Why this company?"** wherever the target puts modern technology (AI especially) into a
  conservative, high-consequence domain — fintech, regtech, insurance, healthcare, govtech.
- **"Why the jump from gaming to ed-tech?"** It answers the discontinuity that reads as
  odd on the CV, by naming what the two roles actually have in common.
- **The AI framing specifically:** agentic AI meeting an institution's rulebook is this same
  problem in a harder form. It positions interest in AI as a career thesis rather than
  attraction to a hot category — which matters with technical founders, who hear the
  difference.

**Pair it with a limitation, not on its own.** In the Factify letter it follows the
concession paragraph (LLM work is pilot-scale; security reviews answered but not fronted as
a vendor). The order matters: conceding first makes the thesis read as self-knowledge
rather than as a pitch.

**"An opportunity to change the product, the tech and the process."** *(Gil's own words,
2026-09-03, on why he wanted the Worldcom Finance CTO role. Reusable wherever the mandate
is broad.)* A "why this role" answer built on **the scope of work available** rather than
on the company's prestige or his own growth. Two parts, and the order matters:

1. **The domain** — stated plainly, as liking it. Not a manufactured mission story.
2. **The breadth of change on offer** — product, technology *and* process, named as three
   things. A candidate who lists all three is describing a mandate rather than a job.

**Why it works.** Most "why this role" answers fail by being about the company's brand or
the candidate's career. This one is about the work, which is the only version a
founder-CEO finds persuasive. It also **pre-answers the disruption question** wherever a
JD or a hiring CEO asks for someone willing to move fast and change things: the appetite
is the reason he applied, so it needs no separate demonstration.

**Always pair it with sequencing judgment.** On its own, "change the product, the tech and
the process" is one step from "change everything," which in a company with a fragile
legacy platform reads as a risk. The pairing: what he would change first, what he would
deliberately leave alone until the platform can absorb it, and how the thing keeps running
while it moves. The CG migration — one on-premise monolith to roughly 200 services with no
availability impact — is the evidence, so the pairing costs a sentence and removes the only
real objection.

**What not to lead with.** Gil's other stated reason — the combination of people and
technology — is true but is what every CTO candidate writes, and most JDs at this level
already name it. Keep it as a closing clause about *this seat* ("architecture, the
organization and the customer loop sit together"), never as the opening reason.

**Distinctiveness theme:** Operating at the *connection points* — domain experts to engineering teams, long-term strategy to what gets built next week, executive judgment to hands-on contribution. Evidence: people who've worked with him repeatedly choose to work with him again (current boss is a former colleague; former team members request to return).

### DORA in prose — the ordering rule

*(Set by Gil, 2026-08-20, writing the Sedric application paragraph. Reusable for
any prose treatment of the DORA claim: application questions, cover letters,
LinkedIn posts, the long-form answer in a room.)*

When the DORA achievement is written out at length rather than stated as a CV
bullet, **order it business impact first, architecture second, tooling not at
all.** The first draft did the opposite — opened on CI/CD, automated coverage and
Haystack across Jira/Jenkins/Git, and arrived at business responsiveness only at
the end. Gil inverted it. The reasons hold generally:

- **Tooling is the least distinctive thing in the story.** Every candidate at this
  level can name a pipeline. Naming one invites the reader to file the whole
  achievement as a DevOps exercise.
- **The architecture beat is the differentiator.** "You cannot put work in front of
  customers several times a day on a system that has to move as one piece" is the
  line that separates a real transformation from a tooling rollout. It says
  deployability was a *design* requirement: modular boundaries, small increments,
  testability and observability built in rather than bolted on, feature toggles
  and abstractions that separate deploying code from launching a capability.
  Expect a technical founder to push here; be ready with where the boundaries were
  drawn, how toggles were governed and retired, and what observability existed at
  the point of release.
- **The four implications, in this order:** (1) the business could absorb a change
  in what the market or a regulator demanded, (2) incidents were rare and quickly
  closed, (3) internal confidence — a delivery commitment stopped being a
  negotiation, which is what sets how ambitious a company is willing to be, and
  (4) the closing note: the business could change its mind, and engineering was
  never the reason it could not.
- **Internal trust is the beat most people omit** and the one Gil rates highest.
  Keep it.

**The trade-off to manage.** Dropping Haystack removes what makes the claim
unfakeable. That is acceptable only when the CV travels with the prose and still
carries "measured continuously via Haystack" on the bullet — then the reader gets
the claim and its substantiation from two documents. If the prose travels alone,
put the instrumentation back in one clause.

**Optional additions**, used only if the specific reader needs them:
- Tier split, in writing: "Elite on deployment frequency and recovery time, high
  tier on lead time and change failure rate." Costs one sentence, buys
  credibility, slows the opening.
- Verification discipline without naming a pipeline: "nothing reached production
  on a human's assurance that it was fine."

**Unverified phrasing to watch.** "A conversation measured in days rather than

## CV Editorial Principles

2. **No "dysfunctional."** Don't denigrate prior teams. Describe what was built, not what it replaced.

4. **Keep Haystack on the page.** Naming the measurement tool makes the DORA claim unfakeable.
5. **Gaming framing: "regulated real-money gaming sector."** Anchors the company, frames technical challenge, less squint factor than "online gaming."
6. **No board-promotion language on the page.** "Promoted to CTO & VP R&D in 2012" is enough. The board detail belongs in conversation.
7. **No "Strangler Fig" on page.** Use "incremental migration patterns" — same meaning, accessible to broader audience.
8. **No "principal" titles.** CET uses "architect," not "principal architect."

10. **No DORA at CET.** Only at CG, where it was earned.
11. **Earlier roles compressed to one line each.** They exist for trajectory, not detail.
12. **Cut Oasis (2005–2008) and SeaPass (2002–2004) entirely.** Pre-2008, irrelevant at this seniority.
13. **First-person summary, third-person bullets.** Voice in summary; executive register in bullets.
14. **Bold lead-ins on the CG bullets only.** Sparingly used for visual rhythm.
15. **Slate-blue section dividers, no flourish.** Dignified, executive.
16. **Target length: one page (US Letter, Calibri 11, ~0.5" top/bottom margins).** Two pages maximum if a target role genuinely needs more detail.
