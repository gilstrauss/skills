# Interview Prep — Stock Answers and Notes

This file holds verbal answers to expected interview questions. The CV makes claims; this file ensures the conversation that follows holds them up.

**Note for Gil:** Several entries below are stubs marked `[Gil to draft]`. Fill these in when you start an active search. The point of writing them down is to think through the framing once, not on the fly under pressure.

## "Tell me about yourself" (the opening question)

Pattern: 90 seconds, three beats.

1. **Who I am now.** "I'm CTO at Center for Educational Technology in Tel Aviv — Israel's leading K-12 ed-tech organization, reaching over a million students. I'm the architect-strategist CTO, focused on technology direction, AI-augmented engineering, and modernizing our legacy platforms."

2. **How I got here.** "Before that, I spent 14 years at CG Solutions / Groove Technologies — joined to build a new team, was promoted to combined CTO and VP R&D about a year in, and over 13 years scaled that organization from 10 to 60 people across R&D, DevOps, IT, NOC, and Product. We built a B2B SaaS platform handling hundreds of millions of daily transactions at 99.99% availability. The part I'm proudest of is the engineering transformation — six years of focused work to reach DORA-elite performance, measured continuously."

3. **What I'm looking for.** *(Confirmed by Gil, 2026-08-19.)*
   > Three things, and they are about the mandate rather than the company. R&D end to end, not a slice of it. Something to build or rebuild rather than maintain. And the authority to take an engineering organization through AI adoption properly — that is the work I am doing now, and I think it is the defining engineering-leadership problem of the next few years. On stage and sector I am genuinely open. I care far more about the mandate than the logo.

   **Why this works:** the openness lands as confidence only because the three non-negotiables arrive first. Never lead with "I'm open to anything" — it reads as availability rather than selectivity, which is precisely the inference the level scatter in the 2024–25 applications already invites.

## "Why are you looking?" (10 months into CET)

**The frame:** fit mismatch between a fast-moving builder and a large, deliberate institution — *not* a falling-out, *not* a criticism of CET. The 14 years at CG is the bulletproof defense against "this is a pattern." Lead with it every time. Let them raise the question; don't volunteer it. Keep it short and unbothered — length signals defensiveness. The word that does the work is **"deliberate"** (respectful; implies "slow" without saying it).

**Full version (~30 seconds):**
> I spent fourteen years at my previous company, so moving isn't something I do lightly. CET is a place I respect — genuinely talented people and a mission I believe in. What these months have clarified for me is where I do my best work: I'm a builder. I'm at my best in high-velocity environments where I can own decisions end-to-end and move fast — break things, iterate, ship. CET is a large, established institution, and by its nature it moves deliberately, with many stakeholders and shifting priorities. That's the right pace for an organization of its size; it's just not the environment where I create the most value. When I saw a role built around exactly that — own the full R&D function, build fast, lead by doing — it was the environment I'm wired for. I'd rather make the honest call than stay somewhere I can't do my best work.

**One-liner:**
> Thirteen years at my last company, so this isn't a pattern. CET is a large, deliberate institution; I'm a builder who's at my best owning R&D end-to-end and moving fast. It's a fit mismatch, not a falling-out — and this role is exactly the environment I'm built for.

**Customize per company:** graft one specific, genuine pull onto the closing sentence (the from-scratch build, the AI-native product, early equity, the domain, a person you know there) so it doesn't read generic.

**Never say:** "disorganized," "bureaucratic," "poor management," "nothing gets done," "they keep changing focus." All accurate; all cost more than they explain.

**Raw underlying reasons (for Gil's eyes only — never speak these):** CET is hectic and hard to move things through; heavy bureaucracy and procedures; priorities shift so often that initiatives stall; the high-churn environment would suit a disruptive startup but works against momentum in a slow-paced institution. The polished answer above is the public translation of these.

## "Why did you leave CG Solutions after 14 years?"

**The facts (from Gil, 2026-08-17):** voluntary departure, April 2025. Fourteen years in, the role felt complete, and he wanted a different business and product to work on.

**The frame:** the work was **finished**, not stalled. This is an achievement-driven exit and it must stay in a different category from the CET answer, which is fit-driven. The word that does the work here is **"finished"** — as "deliberate" does in the CET answer.

**Full version (~30 seconds):**
> I joined at two people and left with sixty. In between, we took the platform from greenfield to hundreds of millions of daily transactions, and spent six years getting the engineering organisation to elite-tier delivery performance. By the start of 2025 that work was finished. Not stalled — finished. The organisation was built, the platform was mature, and what was in front of me was last year's problem in a new financial year. What I wanted next was a different business and a different product to build for: another domain, another model, commercial decisions I hadn't made before. So I left with the work done and on good terms.

**One-liner:**
> Fourteen years, two people to sixty, greenfield to elite-tier delivery. That work was finished, and I wanted a different business and a different product problem to solve.

**The two-move version — use it whenever CG and CET come up together, which is every time:**
> Two decisions, two different reasons. I left CG because the work was finished: fourteen years, two people to sixty, greenfield to elite-tier delivery, and I wanted a different business and product to build for. CET is a different matter. That one is about environment, not achievement — it is a large, deliberate institution, and I am a builder who is at my best owning R&D end to end and moving fast. One was a completed job. The other is a fit mismatch. Neither was a falling-out.

**Follow-up to expect: "So you want to move out of engineering into product or business?"**
> No. I want the technology role with commercial ownership inside it. At CG I had two Heads of Product reporting to me alongside R&D, DevOps, IT and NOC, so I have run that surface. I wanted a different business to run it for, not a different job.

**Never say:**
- **"I wanted to try something new"** (or "something different," "a change," "a new challenge"). This is the honest instinct and it is the single most costly phrase available, because it is also the reason for leaving CET. Said twice, it welds two separate decisions into a pattern and destroys the "fourteen years, so this isn't a pattern" defence. CG is completion; CET is environment. Keep the categories apart.
- **"Fulfilment."** Accurate but soft, and introspective in a way that reads as restlessness on a senior exit. "Finished" carries the same meaning with more spine.
- "Bored," "stagnant," "plateaued," "nothing left to learn." Restlessness is precisely the inference the twelve-month CET tenure already invites; do not feed it.
- Anything implicating the 2025 ownership or management. The 2012 transition is a strength (new owners kept him); hinting that a later one pushed him out contradicts a voluntary exit and invites a reference check on the point.

**The Apr 2025 – Aug 2025 gap.** Four months, visible on LinkedIn, invisible on the CV. *(Confirmed by Gil, 2026-08-19: a planned break. Not a search gap, not consulting.)* With a voluntary departure an unexplained gap invites the suspicion that it was not voluntary, so answer it plainly and move on. The length of the answer signals how much of a problem it is; give it one sentence.

**The frame:** chosen, not endured. The word to lean on is **"planned."** Do not use **"deliberate"** here — that word is load-bearing in the CET answer, and reusing it across both moves welds them together in exactly the way "something new" does.

**Default version (one sentence — resist expanding it):**
> I took four months off on purpose. Fourteen years is a long run, the work was finished in April, and I was not going to pick the next thing on momentum.

**If pressed on what he did with the time** *(filled 2026-09-07 from Gil: "mostly vacation, then get some rest between the next job. I did seek a job but not intensively.")*:
> Mostly time off — travel and family, deliberately. Fourteen years without a proper break earns four months. I kept an eye on the market while I was out, but I was not in a hurry, and I took the CET role in August.

**The ordering rule, and it is load-bearing.** Gil's own account includes "I did seek a job." That is true and is not to be denied — but it must not lead. Rest was the purpose; the looking was incidental and unhurried. **"I kept an eye on the market" is the correct register for *not intensively*** — it neither denies the search nor promotes it to the headline. Leading with the search converts a chosen break into a four-month unsuccessful one, which is precisely the inference a short current tenure already invites.

**If pressed harder — "so you were looking for four months?"** Answer plainly and unbothered:
> Not really looking, no — not in any organised way. I was resting, and I was open to hearing about something good. The CET conversation started in the summer and I took it. If I had been urgently looking I would have been in a job by May.

That last sentence is the whole defence, and it is unarguable: a candidate who wanted a job in April 2025, with that CV, would have had one within weeks. **The length of the break is evidence he was not desperate, not evidence he was unwanted** — provided he sounds unbothered saying so.

**Never say:**
- **"I was looking"** as the *opening* of the answer, or "it took a while to find the right thing." The first inverts the true order of priorities; the second asserts a difficulty he did not have. Both hand over the pattern reading for free. Note this is an ordering rule, not a denial — see above.
- **"Recharge," "burnt out," "needed a rest."** At this level this reads as capacity rather than judgement, and it contradicts the "finished" frame, which is an achievement claim.
- **"Deliberate."** Reserved for the CET answer. See above.
- Anything that stretches or shrinks the four months. The dates are on LinkedIn and the interviewer can see them.

**CV consequence — this is the part that matters beyond the interview.** The answer only lands if the CV shows commercial thinking, and it currently carries no business outcome at all (see `rejection_analysis_2026-08-17.md`, section 6c: every metric on the page is transactions, uptime, DORA or headcount). "I wanted a different business to build for," said over a page that never mentions revenue, cost or time-to-market, reads as a preference rather than a capability. Putting one commercial number on the page — the AWS cost reduction is the obvious candidate — is what converts this answer from plausible to evidenced.

## "How did you measure DORA-elite?"

> Haystack, integrated with Jira, Jenkins, and Git — continuous instrumentation across the full delivery pipeline. We hit elite-tier on deployment frequency and MTTR. High-tier — one grade below elite — on lead time for changes and change failure rate. That profile, where you ship fast and recover fast but your planning front-end is slightly slower, is common at organizations that prioritized operational excellence. We owned it and worked on the lead-time side throughout. The transformation took six years end to end.

## "What were your direct reports at CG Solutions?"

> Five at peak: Head of Product for our main line of business, Head of Product for the second line of business, R&D Manager, Head of DevOps and IT, and the NOC Manager. The NOC piece is unusual at this scope — most CTOs at a 60-person org don't have 24/7 operations under them. We did, because the platform couldn't go down.

## "How hands-on are you, really?"

> Hands-on means something different in 2026 than it did five years ago. I work with coding agents — Claude Code, Codex, Cursor — writing specs, reviewing designs, reviewing code, pairing on hard problems with AI agents and engineers. I don't type every line, but the production output is genuinely mine in a meaningful sense. I'm in the architecture, I'm in the trade-offs, I'm in the code reviews. If you're asking whether I can defend a system design at the whiteboard, the answer is yes. If you're asking whether I'd be a Staff Engineer dressed up as a CTO, the answer is no — I'm a CTO who actually engages with the work.

## "When could you start?" / "What's your notice period?"

**The facts** *(confirmed by Gil, 2026-09-11)*: **thirty days by contract at CET**; he would
accept **sixty as a courtesy** if CET asks for longer to hand over.

> Thirty days contractually. If CET asks for longer to hand over properly I'd want to
> accommodate that, so call it thirty to sixty depending on where things stand — but thirty is
> the obligation.

**Lead with thirty. Never open on sixty.** Opening on the courtesy figure makes sixty the date
the employer plans around, giving away a month he doesn't owe for nothing in return. State the
obligation first; offer the flexibility second.

**Keep the courtesy conditional.** "If they ask" is accurate; "I'll be giving them sixty days"
is a commitment he hasn't made to CET and shouldn't volunteer to a company that hasn't made an
offer.

**One sentence, then stop.** A long answer about handover arrangements reads as anxiety about
how CET will take the resignation. The clause is worth including because it gives a date to
diary and signals someone who leaves places well — quietly useful alongside a short-tenure
explanation.

## "What questions do you have for us?"

Always have 3–5 ready. Vary by company; some perennial good ones:
- "What's the technical decision the team got most wrong in the last two years, and what did you learn from it?"
- "What does the first 90 days look like for whoever takes this role?"
- "If I asked the engineers on this team what they'd change about how the org works, what would they say?"
- "What's the metric you'd use to know I'm succeeding six months in?"
- "What's the part of this role you're most worried about filling?"

## Notes on stylistic delivery

- **On modesty:** Gil's track record is strong. He doesn't need to oversell. Stating facts (60 people, DORA-elite, hundreds of millions of transactions) in a flat tone is more credible than enthusiasm.
- **On the gaming industry:** Don't apologize for it. The technical achievement transfers; gaming was a great training ground for high-volume, regulated, real-money systems.
- **On the connection-points distinctiveness:** Don't say "I'm good at the connection points" — show it through the answers. The CTO who codes is different from the CTO who only sets strategy; the leader whose former colleagues hire him again is different from the leader who burns bridges.
